# PSY-A-01 — Differentiated story options and chosen complete treatment
**Status:** STORY TREATMENT CANDIDATE, not founder approved. Based on [source research](research.md) and the account [audience gate](brief.md), not on the old 27-second opening.

## Three genuinely different ways to tell this
### A — The algorithm that crowned a shed
- **Question:** Can someone exploit restaurant rankings without a restaurant?
- **First image:** A ranking dashboard counts down until #1; hard cut to a shabby shed.
- **Spine:** Fake business listing → fake reviews → climb → #1 → TripAdvisor response.
- **Strength:** Fast and high-concept; clean, economical visuals.
- **Weakness:** Ends on a number, mostly attacks a platform, underuses human behavior and the richest payoff. Audience leaves amused, less perceptive.

### B — The restaurant nobody can book
- **Question:** What if refusing every customer makes you famous?
- **First image:** A phone rings; a disappointed would-be diner is told no tables are available.
- **Spine:** Hidden location → fake online buzz → caller refusals → self-reinforcing mystique → crush of inquiries → reveal.
- **Strength:** Distinctive everyday human situation; scarcity dramatized rather than lectured.
- **Weakness:** Could overstate evidence that refusals caused the rise or ignore that the ranking was driven by reviews.

### C — The proof machine (**recommended**)
- **Question:** When a restaurant has reviews, ranking, demand and apparently happy diners, how could the whole thing still be a shed?
- **First image:** A real reservation inquiry, treated as important; reveal the shed.
- **Spine:** Oobah knows fake reviews work → creates a fake listing and fabricated food → recruits glowing reviews → ranking climbs → real interest and manufactured scarcity build on the listing → #1 → must actually open → some real diners don't buy it while others ask to return → trace the apparently separate credibility signals back to the same orchestrated hoax.
- **Strength:** The *events themselves* teach the Psychology/Power/Influence account's desired perceptiveness; provides an earned end after the #1 reveal, real people and messy evidence rather than a simplistic “everyone is gullible.”
- **Weakness:** More scenes, higher runtime than a 45-second microshort. Must be cut sharply and avoid pretending an anecdote is a controlled psychological demonstration.

## Selected treatment C — full beginning / middle / end
**Audience payoff:** Viewers gain a concrete instinct to ask whether many credibility cues come from independent evidence or one manipulated source. Emotion: amused disbelief first, recognition second. Buyer fit remains hypothetical.

**Act 1 — The impossible reservation (setup; ~0:00–0:35):**
A phone rings at a supposedly highly sought-after restaurant; caller told full for six weeks. Wide reveal: it's a shed. Establish Oobah and his firsthand experience writing paid-for bogus reviews. His experiment: make even the restaurant itself fictional. This makes the odd premise causal (he knows the weakness) and gives the audience a question.

**Act 2 — Build a restaurant out of cues (~0:35–1:35):**
A £10 burner phone, a website, street-only address and appointment-only positioning. Invented mood menu with photos of an egg on his foot and painted sponge/shaving foam, executed like gourmet. Real listing gets approved; friends write reviews; ranking climbs from #18,149 to #1. While it rises, real calls, suppliers, applicants and PR offers surface. Whenever Oobah says “fully booked,” the lack of a table becomes part of the attraction. Each new beat either adds a credibility cue or escalates the consequence.

**Act 3 — Reality catches up (~1:35–2:15):**
Oobah faces real customers demanding a restaurant. He decides to open for one night. Bring some diners blindfolded into a garden; surround them with actors praising meals and a DJ providing restaurant sound while microwave-ready meals masquerade as haute cuisine. The question changes from *can the internet be fooled?* to *what happens when someone experiences the real thing?*

**Act 4 — Contradictory responses and the reveal (~2:15–2:40):**
One couple appears unimpressed; another table asks about booking again, both per Butler's firsthand account. Avoid stating that all guests were tricked; the dinner was free and artificial. The final visual reverses the credibility chain: fake reviews → algorithmic ranking → demand → manufactured scarcity → planted social cues. What appeared to be multiple distinct reasons to trust a business began with one coordinated hoax. Last image returns to the very ordinary shed; opening premise and thematic promise paid off.

## Why this length
~330 spoken words needs roughly 2:00–2:15 at a brisk 150–165 words/minute, plus visual reveals, pauses and performance; the candidate **complete episode** should be tested near 2:15–2:45. We do not artificially stretch it to 3–5 minutes or crush it into an incomplete 27-second teaser. If approved, one 15–30-second coherent segment can be used **only** to test animation quality, not mistaken for a finished episode.

## Three highest-risk creative compromises
1. **Too many phone/ranking/UI graphics:** favor two decisive comedy objects and a legible, compact progression instead of 50 generic text notifications.
2. **Didactic finish:** use the visual reversal of independent-looking signals; don't lecture on “cognitive biases” in abstract terms.
3. **Causation oversold:** actual guests reacted differently; ranking date has contradictory accounts; staged actors and free dinner do not prove any precise psychological effect.
