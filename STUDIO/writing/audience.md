# Audience psychology and meaningful relevance
Sources: active `../audience-research.md`, `../creative-os.md`, `../editorial-workflow.md`; original Creative OS §§3, 13, 17–18. Read the relevant account and episode briefs too.

Identify one specific viewer job: what does this person want to understand, protect, avoid, or become? Include the uncomfortable motives where relevant—embarrassment, exclusion, resentment, status, dependence—as hypotheses unless real audience evidence supports them. Never invent audience quotes or universal motivations.

Choose one primary emotional experience and a supporting contrast. Curiosity can lead to recognition; tension can release into dry humor. Do not try to trigger every emotion or keep intensity permanently high.

Make relevance part of the situation. A person selecting a birthday venue has a concrete decision; saying “imagine how embarrassed you'd be” invents a feeling and tells the audience how to respond. Show the documented stakes, then leave room for recognition. Avoid flattering viewers as smarter than the people in the story.

The story must reward attention beyond being unusual. Define the insight before drafting and identify the events that earn it. Distinguish an interpretation from a proven psychological mechanism. One anecdote does not establish why every participant acted as they did.

For commercial fit, distinguish entertainment viewers from buyers. State what further learning or practice could naturally help this audience, and what evidence is missing. Do not insert a sales pitch for an unavailable product. Return interest should arise from a useful, satisfying episode—not an artificially withheld answer.
