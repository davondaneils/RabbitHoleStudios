# Hooks that are clear and repay their promise
Sources: original Creative OS §§2–4, 9–10, 19; active `../creative-os.md`.

A stranger should quickly understand the situation, the interesting contradiction or decision, and a reason to continue. Start with a concrete event or recognizable choice. Avoid introductions, vague warnings, unexplained pronouns, branding and withheld basic context.

Choose an approach that fits the actual story: an unexpected result, a familiar decision going wrong, a visible contradiction, a consequential obstacle, or an unanswered question. These are alternatives, not ingredients to combine in every opening.

Give enough orientation to make the surprise meaningful. “He'd arranged those too” fails if the listener doesn't yet know who he is or what “those” means. Naming the thing is often clearer than preserving mystery.

A hook creates an obligation. Write its promised payoff beside the outline; confirm that the full episode actually delivers it. Revealing the headline outcome can still work if the story offers a compelling explanation or consequence. Do not rely on withholding the ending or a fabricated threat.

Reject an opening if it needs a reread, sounds more dramatic than the evidence, requires a previous episode, or promises a different story. Do not make it “punchier” by breaking a clear sentence into disconnected fragments.

First-frame clarity must eventually be checked in the actual visual. A text hook alone cannot prove phone-size legibility, watch time, or retention. Prefer one readable visual idea over dense captions or a logo sequence.
