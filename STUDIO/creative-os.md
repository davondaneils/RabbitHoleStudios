# Active Creative Operating System

**This is a palette, not a checklist.** The original 50-section guide lives in `ARCHIVE/creative-os-v1.md`, but this shorter file is the active writing standard.

## Required craft modules

Use [the writing library](writing/README.md) and its task-routing table. Full scripts and substantive rewrites require audience, hooks, story, narration and editing modules. The modules bring selected deeper craft guidance into active use; the archive remains historical. For Instagram, connected, instantly understandable narration takes priority over punchy fragmentation.

## Audience value before the spine (mandatory)

For each episode, first read the relevant account brief and `STUDIO/audience-research.md`, then complete the [Audience Value Gate and editorial stages](editorial-workflow.md). Establish **which specific viewer desire this story serves, the entertaining/emotional payoff, the useful insight or recognition, and any unvalidated buyer connection**. The psychology/insight should emerge from documented events—not be bolted on as a moral in the last sentence. After that, apply the writing craft palette below. A great standalone oddity is not necessarily a great episode for the chosen account.

**Full episode first:** develop a complete cause-and-effect treatment and script before extracting 15–30 seconds for animation testing. Distinguish opening fragments from full episodes. Make the full script undergo a separate skeptical editorial pass and founder approval before storyboarding.

## Four core writing instructions

1. **One dramatic spine:** Make one meaningful point, promise or emotional experience. A hook raises an expectation the video actually repays.
2. **Real human texture:** Give viewers actions, material details, ordinary social friction, recognizable dialogue and specific images instead of vague universal labels.
3. **An evolving story:** Each included beat changes what happens, what we believe, the stakes, a choice or a relationship. Cause and effect beat arbitrary motion.
4. **Ruthless economy:** Select only useful craft tools. If a sentence, example, scene, transition or visual can be removed without weakening the result, remove it.

## Optional craft tools (choose; never mandate them all)

- In medias res, anomaly, dilemma, contrast, interrupted action, surprising evidence, strong question, cold open.
- Curiosity, suspense, dramatic irony, status reversal, fear, anger, guilt, aspiration, tenderness, pride, humor, awe, belonging, relief.
- Stakes and constraints; agency/choice; specific antagonists; contradiction; cognitive dissonance; personal recognition.
- Dialogue with subtext; interior perspective; silence; reaction shots; recurring symbols; visual metaphors; cliffhanger *after* local payoff.
- Associative leaps, jump cuts, time ellipses, fragments, parataxis, free indirect discourse, juxtaposition and rhythm shifts.
- Serial arcs, callbacks, framing-device conversations, story cutaways, scenes within scenes, recurring worlds.
- Real-world referents (actual products, objects, places, public figures, events, idioms) where they genuinely fit. Never random brand-name packing.
- Editing tools: sound hit, silence, match cut, contrast, frame hold, camera movement, motion graphics, caption, diagrams, performance animation.

## Human-first scripting process

**1. Build the spine.** Write: 'This is about [specific person/choice/problem], and by the end the viewer will [learn/feel/see/decide] ____.' Choose only primary emotion + useful contrast. Define payoff now.

**2. Research and feed real texture.** Source facts, true story, original audience language, physical settings, plausible props, characteristic speech, idioms, relevant cultural references, and specific tradeoffs. Mark recreated dialogue as fictional. Do not claim fake anecdotes are real.

**3. Make it a scene.** Who wants what? What blocks them? What physically happens? What changes? Use one telling object over five generic adjectives. 'Show, don't tell' where action or expression can carry the meaning. Narration is allowed if it creates clarity or saves irrelevant footage.

**4. Draft freely.** Don't write as an essay with a tidy moral conclusion in every paragraph. Use conversational fragments, abrupt transitions, mixed line lengths, thought, action, visual association and silence *when appropriate*. Different characters should sound different.

**5. De-AI micro pass.** Find smooth generic phrases ('be present', 'embrace your journey', 'in today's world'), endless 'not X but Y', symmetrical triple lists, manufactured inspirational endings, repeated explanations, and unnatural or sterile dialogue. Replace abstractions with precise lived action (e.g., 'you've watched YouTube since breakfast; Uncle Mike gets here in twenty minutes, help me with the grill') when the detail strengthens the scene. Don't overdo artificial 'human messiness'.

**6. Whole-piece coherence pass.** Ignore how nice each sentence sounds. Does every example, metaphor and real-world detail support the *same* narrative experience? Avoid competing analogical worlds and collage-like name-dropping. Associative jumps need an intuitive emotional, causal, sensory or thematic bridge. An intentional change of metaphor can work; mechanical perfect consistency is not the goal.

**7. Subtraction.** Cut redundant facts, one-too-many analogies, separate micro-payoffs competing with the main payoff, forced villains, forced lessons and obligatory cliffhangers. Editing is about having nothing unnecessary left, not maximizing techniques.

**8. Read it aloud and visualize.** Listen through the actual VO voice. Can a stranger follow while mildly distracted? Would a shot, expression or pause outperform a sentence? Does the opening make one legitimate promise and the ending honor it? Quality and source check before animation.

## Short-form storytelling principles

- The first frame must be legible on a phone, even before viewers know the series.
- A 25-second video can beat a padded 60-second video. Choose length for the idea, not quota.
- Cliffhangers can drive serial watching but do not substitute for delivering the local promised payoff.
- Newcomers should understand an episode without needing Part 1; returning viewers may get additional character rewards.
- The framing-device structure is one tested tool: recurring characters pose a question -> dramatized story/historical analogy -> return to characters for payoff. Don't impose it on every show.
- Long-form-first modular writing is another unproven distribution experiment: write a complete piece with meaningful Reel/Short boundaries; do not arbitrarily chop into unfinished chunks.
- Humor, sincerity, horror, anger, stillness and complexity can all be valid. Emotional intensity is not automatically depth.

## Final creative review: five questions, not a 50-item scorecard

1. Why would a stranger stop?
2. What exactly is happening and why does it matter?
3. What genuinely changes as it unfolds?
4. Does the payoff satisfy the promise?
5. Can anything be removed without making the whole weaker?

Then separately veto factual fabrications, unsafe advice, rights issues and materially misleading reenactments. Quality scoring and retention charts guide revisions; they are not magic algorithm laws.
